# #90 Research: evolution and spread of life as a second simulation phase

Sources and findings for [plan.md](plan.md). The goal is an **Earth-like planet, not Earth**:
Earth's history is one realisation and serves as a plausibility check, not a script. Grid-size
effects (~110 km cells at 6 subdivisions, ~220 km on the web) are accepted (#64).

Reading depth is marked per source: **[full]** read in full or in large part earlier/now,
**[abstract]** only the abstract, publisher page or a reliable summary was checked in this task,
**[known]** a standard reference cited from background knowledge, bibliographic data checked
where a link is given. Numbers marked "≈" are rounded consensus values that differ between

## 1. Key transitions of Earth's biosphere and what controlled them

| Transition | Earth age (≈) | Controls / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin of life, first microbial ecosystems | ≥ 3.5 Ga (stromatolites 3.43–3.48 Ga; claims to 3.7–4.1 Ga disputed) | Liquid water, energy gradients (hydrothermal, photic). Life appears almost as soon as the surface is habitable. |
| Oxygenic photosynthesis | ≥ 2.7–3.0 Ga (contested) | Origin poorly constrained; O₂ consumed by reduced sinks for hundreds of Myr. |
| Great Oxidation Event (GOE) | 2.4–2.3 Ga | Atmospheric O₂ from < 10⁻⁵ PAL to ~10⁻³–10⁻¹ PAL; deep ocean stays anoxic for ~2 Gyr more. Coincides with Palaeoproterozoic (Huronian) glaciations. |
| Eukaryotes (crown group) | ~1.8–1.6 Ga (stem earlier) | Need some O₂; remain ecologically minor for ~1 Gyr ("boring billion", low mid-Proterozoic O₂, perhaps < 0.1–1% PAL). |
| Rise of eukaryotic algae | ~0.66–0.64 Ga | After the Sturtian snowball: nutrient (P) pulse from glacial weathering; shifts marine food webs (steranes). |
| Snowball Earth episodes | 0.72–0.66 Ga, 0.65–0.635 Ga | Global glaciations ended by volcanic CO₂ build-up; followed by cap carbonates and nutrient surges. |
| Neoproterozoic Oxygenation Event | ~0.8–0.54 Ga | O₂ rises towards modern levels, deep ocean oxygenates (patchily, into the Palaeozoic). |
| Ediacaran macrobiota / first animals | ~0.575–0.54 Ga (sponges perhaps earlier) | Large multicellular life; O₂ thresholds for animals of a few % PAL (sponges) to tens of % (active predators). |
| Cambrian radiation | 0.539–0.51 Ga | Most animal phyla appear within ~20–30 Myr; predation, burrowing, biomineralisation. |
| Land plants (embryophytes) | origin mid-Cambrian–Early Ordovician (molecular clock, ~0.5 Ga); spores ~0.47 Ga | Evolved from freshwater charophyte algae: colonisation from wet margins inland. |
| Vascular plants | Late Ordovician–Silurian (~0.43 Ga) | Taller plants, roots, enhanced weathering. |
| Forests | Middle–Late Devonian (~0.39–0.36 Ga) | Deep roots; drawdown of CO₂, soil formation, change of rivers (meandering); Late Devonian / Carboniferous glaciation. |
| Grasses, C4 | Cenozoic (C4 expansion ~8–6 Ma) | Earth-specific detail, not needed. |

Sources:

- Knoll & Nowak (2017), "The timetable of evolution", *Science Advances* 3, e1603076,
  [doi:10.1126/sciadv.1603076](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1603076) **[abstract]**. The GOE
  and the Neoproterozoic Oxygenation Event are the main pacemakers of early evolution: the first
  enabling eukaryotes, the second large complex multicellular life. Later innovations are paced by
  biology (regulatory networks, ecological change) rather than by the environment.
- Lyons, Reinhard & Planavsky (2014), "The rise of oxygen in Earth's early ocean and atmosphere",
  *Nature* 506, 307–315, [doi:10.1038/nature13068](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13068)
  **[abstract]**. Stepwise oxygenation: < 0.001% PAL before the GOE, low mid-Proterozoic levels,
  deep-ocean oxygenation lagging by almost 2 Gyr.
- Planavsky et al. (2014), "Low Mid-Proterozoic atmospheric oxygen levels and the delayed rise of
  animals", *Science* 346, 635–638,
  [doi:10.1126/science.1258410](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1258410) **[known]**.
- Sperling et al. (2013), "Oxygen, ecology, and the Cambrian radiation of animals", *PNAS* 110,
  13446–13451, [doi:10.1073/pnas.1312778110](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1312778110)
  **[known]**. Low O₂ limits carnivory; the O₂ rise as a permissive trigger of the radiation.
- Hoffman et al. (2017), "Snowball Earth climate dynamics and Cryogenian geology–geobiology",
  *Science Advances* 3, e1600983,
  [doi:10.1126/sciadv.1600983](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1600983) **[known]**.
- Brocks et al. (2017), "The rise of algae in Cryogenian oceans and the emergence of animals",
  *Nature* 548, 578–581, [doi:10.1038/nature23457](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23457)
  **[known]**. Post-snowball nutrient pulse → algal takeover → food for animals.
- Betts et al. (2018), "Integrated genomic and fossil evidence illuminates life's early
  evolution and eukaryote origin", *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 2, 1556–1562,
  [doi:10.1038/s41559-018-0644-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-018-0644-x) **[known]**.
- Morris et al. (2018), "The timescale of early land plant evolution", *PNAS* 115, E2274–E2283,
  [doi:10.1073/pnas.1719588115](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1719588115) **[abstract]**. Land
  plants originated mid-Cambrian to Early Ordovician, vascular plants Late Ordovician–Silurian.
- Lenton et al. (2012), "First plants cooled the Ordovician", *Nature Geoscience* 5, 86–89,
  [doi:10.1038/ngeo1390](https://doi.org/10.1038/ngeo1390) **[abstract + press summaries]**.
  Non-vascular plants (moss experiments) enhance silicate weathering and P release; in a
  carbon-cycle model this draws CO₂ down enough to trigger the Late Ordovician glaciation, even
  with only 5–15% of the land covered.
- Stein et al. (2007), "Giant cladoxylopsid trees resolve the enigma of the Earth's earliest
  forest stumps at Gilboa", *Nature* 446, 904–907,
  [doi:10.1038/nature05705](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05705) **[known]** (forests by the
  Middle Devonian).
- Davies & Gibling (2010), Gibling & Davies (2012), McMahon & Davies (2018): pre-vegetation
  braided rivers and scarce mudrock; already cited in `src/presets.rs` (`Biosphere::Abiotic`).

**Findings for the transition.**

1. The first ~3 Gyr of life are microbial and act on the planet as **bulk processes**
   (weathering, mats, O₂ and CH₄ in the atmosphere, carbonate and pelagic sediment). The present
   presets already capture the land side of this as `--biosphere abiotic` (bare land, braided
   rivers, no biogenic pelagic ooze) and the faint Sun / compensating greenhouse as `--epoch`.
2. What changes the **physical** planet in a way the current model can see, and what makes
   spatially resolved biology interesting, is the **colonisation of land** (albedo,
   evapotranspiration, root cohesion, weathering/CO₂), together with complex marine life on
   shelves (reefs, biogenic carbonate). On Earth these follow each other within ~100 Myr
   (0.54 → 0.47 → 0.39 Ga).
3. The **timing is permissive, not deterministic**: oxygenation steps and snowball exits open
   windows; which window life uses, and when, is contingent (Knoll & Nowak 2017). Earth needed
   ~4.0 Gyr to reach it; nothing in physics fixes that number for an Earth-like planet. A model
   without an O₂ cycle cannot predict the date honestly, so the trigger should be a chosen
   parameter, optionally gated by conditions the model *does* have (no snowball; see plan.md
   §2.2 for why a run time is preferred over a planet age in this model).
4. Snowball exits and nutrient pulses precede both the algal rise and the Cambrian. The model can
   detect a snowball (frozen fraction) but has no P cycle; a "habitable shelf with nutrient
   supply" map is the closest proxy it can produce.

## 2. Mechanistic macroevolution and biogeography models

- **gen3sis** — Hagen et al. (2021), "gen3sis: A general engine for eco-evolutionary simulations
  of the processes that shape Earth's biodiversity", *PLoS Biology* 19, e3001340,
  [doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.3001340](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001340),
  [PMC8384074](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8384074) **[abstract + earlier familiarity
  with the method]**. Spatially explicit, R package (C++ core). Each species: a set of occupied
  cells with per-population trait values (e.g. thermal optimum). Per time step: (1) environment
  update; (2) **speciation** — populations of a species split into geographic clusters (cells
  within a dispersal distance); clusters accumulate divergence while separated and become new
  species past a threshold, merge back when they reconnect; (3) **dispersal** — random dispersal
  distances from a kernel, colonising suitable cells; (4) **trait evolution** — Brownian/OU-like
  drift and homogenisation within clusters; (5) **ecology** — per-cell abundance from the fit of
  traits to the environment and a cell carrying capacity shared by competitors; species with zero
  abundance in a cell go locally extinct, globally extinct when no cell is left. Outputs: α/β/γ
  diversity, range sizes, traits, phylogenies. Demonstrated on the Cenozoic latitudinal diversity
  gradient. Costs grow with species × occupied cells and with the pairwise distance matrices for
  clustering; their runs used coarse grids (1–4° cells) and capped the number of species.
- **SPLIT** — Descombes et al. (2018), "Linking species diversification to palaeo-environmental
  changes: A process-based modelling approach", *Global Ecology and Biogeography* 27, 233–244,
  [doi:10.1111/geb.12683](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12683) **[abstract]**. Neutral-ish lineage
  model on reconstructed palaeo-habitats: ecologically identical, non-interacting species;
  speciation when a range is fragmented, extinction when habitat disappears, dispersal limited by
  distance. Reproduced mangrove richness gradients. Shows that **habitat geometry alone** (from a
  tectonic/climate model) already yields realistic diversity patterns.
- Leprieur et al. (2016), "Plate tectonics drive tropical reef biodiversity dynamics", *Nature
  Communications* 7, 11461, [doi:10.1038/ncomms11461](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms11461)
  **[abstract]**. The marine predecessor: shallow tropical reef habitat from plate reconstructions
  over 140 Myr; parapatric speciation by habitat fragmentation; reproduces the movement of
  hotspots (Tethys → Indo-Australian Archipelago). Directly analogous to what this simulator can
  do with its own plates and shelves.
- Rangel et al. (2018), "Modeling the ecology and evolution of biodiversity: Biogeographical
  cradles, museums, and graves", *Science* 361, eaar5452,
  [doi:10.1126/science.aar5452](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar5452) **[abstract]**. South
  America over 800 kyr with adaptation, range shifts, fragmentation, speciation, dispersal,
  competition, extinction driven by modelled climates; emerging richness maps resemble real ones
  without fitting. Climate *change* (glacial cycles) drives both speciation and extinction.
- Neutral theory (Hubbell 2001, *The Unified Neutral Theory of Biodiversity and Biogeography*,
  Princeton) and spatially explicit coalescent/neutral models **[known]**: diversity from
  speciation–drift–dispersal balance; useful as the null model for a cheap lineage layer.
- Daisyworld — Watson & Lovelock (1983), *Tellus B* 35, 284–289,
  [doi:10.1111/j.1600-0889.1983.tb00031.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0889.1983.tb00031.x)
  **[known]**: the minimal coupled life–albedo–temperature model; shows that trait-based life
  coupled to albedo regulates climate, and the stability pitfalls of such feedbacks.

**Findings.** All successful models of this family (a) represent species as **sets of cells**
plus a few traits, not individuals; (b) get speciation from **range fragmentation** (allopatry),
the only speciation mode that a ~100 km grid can resolve; (c) are driven by **environmental
change** — a static landscape quickly reaches a speciation–extinction equilibrium with little
further structure. (c) is the strongest argument against freezing the continents for long.
Their time steps are 10³–10⁵ yr for climate-cycle studies, but speciation itself takes ~0.1–few
Myr, so Myr steps are adequate when the forcing (tectonics, slow climate) is Myr-scale.

## 3. Vegetation models (DGVM-lite) and biome classification

- **Holdridge life zones** — Holdridge (1947), "Determination of world plant formations from
  simple climatic data", *Science* 105, 367–368,
  [doi:10.1126/science.105.2727.367](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.105.2727.367) **[known]**:
  biotemperature, precipitation, PET ratio on log axes.
- **Whittaker biomes** — Whittaker (1975), *Communities and Ecosystems*, Macmillan **[known]**:
  biomes on annual mean temperature × annual precipitation. Simplest; 8–9 classes.
- **Köppen–Geiger** — Kottek et al. (2006), "World map of the Köppen-Geiger climate
  classification updated", *Meteorologische Zeitschrift* 15, 259–263,
  [doi:10.1127/0941-2948/2006/0130](https://doi.org/10.1127/0941-2948/2006/0130) **[known]**.
  Needs monthly temperature and precipitation — both available in the model
  (`TemperatureFields::monthly`, `WaterCycle::monthly_precipitation`).
- **BIOME** — Prentice et al. (1992), "A global biome model based on plant physiology and
  dominance, soil properties and climate", *Journal of Biogeography* 19, 117–134,
  [doi:10.2307/2845499](https://doi.org/10.2307/2845499) **[known]**: plant functional types (PFTs)
  with bioclimatic limits (coldest-month T, growing degree days, moisture index α = AET/PET);
  the dominant PFT combination gives the biome. The natural middle ground between a lookup table
  and a DGVM, and uses exactly the quantities the model computes.
- **LPJ** — Sitch et al. (2003), "Evaluation of ecosystem dynamics, plant geography and
  terrestrial carbon cycling in the LPJ dynamic global vegetation model", *Global Change Biology*
  9, 161–185, [doi:10.1046/j.1365-2486.2003.00569.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2486.2003.00569.x)
  **[known]**: 10 PFTs, daily/monthly photosynthesis, water balance, fire, establishment and
  mortality, carbon pools. Too detailed for Myr steps; its structure (PFT bioclimatic limits +
  NPP + fractional cover) is what a lite version keeps.
- **Miami model** — Lieth (1975), "Modeling the primary productivity of the world", in Lieth &
  Whittaker (eds.), *Primary Productivity of the Biosphere*, Springer **[known]**: NPP =
  min(f(T), f(P)) with f(T) = 3000/(1+e^(1.315−0.119T)), f(P) = 3000(1−e^(−0.000664P)) g/m²/yr
  (dry matter). Del Grosso et al. (2008), *Ecology* 89, 2117–2126,
  [doi:10.1890/07-0850.1](https://doi.org/10.1890/07-0850.1) **[known]**: updated empirical NPP
  from T and P. Land NPP ≈ 55–60 Pg C/yr, ocean ≈ 45–50 (Field et al. 1998, *Science* 281,
  237–240, [doi:10.1126/science.281.5374.237](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.281.5374.237)
  **[known]**).

**Findings.** A PFT scheme à la BIOME with fractional cover and a Miami-style NPP needs only
annual/monthly T, P, PET and AET — all already computed per cell and month — and costs
O(cells × PFTs). Biomes then follow as a display classification (Whittaker or Köppen as a
cross-check) instead of driving physics.

## 4. Vegetation–climate feedbacks

- **Albedo**: Charney (1975), "Dynamics of deserts and drought in the Sahel", *QJRMS* 101,
  193–202, [doi:10.1002/qj.49710142802](https://doi.org/10.1002/qj.49710142802) **[known]**:
  albedo–subsidence feedback of desert margins. Bonan (2008), "Forests and climate change",
  *Science* 320, 1444–1449, [doi:10.1126/science.1155121](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1155121)
  **[known]**: boreal forests warm (snow masking, low albedo), tropical forests cool (ET).
  Snow-covered forest albedo ~0.2–0.3 vs. snow-covered tundra ~0.6–0.8. Bare land 0.3–0.4,
  vegetated 0.15–0.2 (already in `presets.rs`).
- **Evapotranspiration and precipitation recycling**: Kleidon, Fraedrich & Heimann (2000), "A
  green planet versus a desert world: Estimating the maximum effect of vegetation on the land
  surface climate", *Climatic Change* 44, 471–493,
  [doi:10.1023/A:1005559518889](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1005559518889) **[abstract]**: land ET
  more than triples and land precipitation doubles between a desert and a maximally green planet
  (prescribed SSTs). The current abiotic preset reproduces the sign and a smaller magnitude (−35%
  land rain) via Fu's ω (Budyko); per-cell ω from vegetation is the natural generalisation.
- **CO₂ / silicate weathering**: Walker, Hays & Kasting (1981), *JGR* 86, 9776–9782,
  [doi:10.1029/JC086iC10p09776](https://doi.org/10.1029/JC086iC10p09776) **[known]**, and Berner's
  GEOCARB models (e.g. Berner 2006, "GEOCARBSULF", *GCA* 70, 5653–5664,
  [doi:10.1016/j.gca.2005.11.032](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2005.11.032) **[known]**): weathering
  flux = f(T, runoff, uplift/erosion, plant factor) balancing volcanic outgassing on ≥ 0.1–1 Myr
  scales. Vascular plants raise weathering by a factor ~2–10 in field comparisons (Moulton &
  Berner 1998, *Mineralogical Magazine* 62A, 1030–1031,
  [abstract](https://rruff.info/doclib/MinMag/Volume_62A/62A-2-1030.htm) **[abstract]**: birch vs.
  bare basalt in Iceland, ×8 Ca, up to ×27 Mg+K+Na). COPSE: Lenton et al. (2018), "COPSE
  reloaded", *Earth-Science Reviews* 178, 1–28,
  [doi:10.1016/j.earscirev.2017.12.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.earscirev.2017.12.004)
  **[known]**: box model of C, O, P, S over the Phanerozoic with a land-biota weathering factor.
  Maher & Chamberlain (2014), *Science* 343, 1502–1504,
  [doi:10.1126/science.1250770](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1250770) **[known]**: weathering
  limited by runoff and supply (erosion) — the model has both fields.
  The timescale (~0.1–1 Myr to rebalance) means a **quasi-steady CO₂ per 1-Myr step** is adequate.
- **Erosion**: vegetation cover reduces hillslope erosion steeply and changes river style
  (Langbein & Schumm 1958; Vanacker et al. 2007; Gibling & Davies 2012 — in `presets.rs`). Already
  parameterised globally as K×2 and sediment diffusivity 10⁴ vs 5×10³ m²/yr for abiotic land.

## 5. What the model already has (code survey, `main` at 67ca0a2)

- `src/presets.rs`: `Biosphere::{Modern, Abiotic}` changes four *global* scalars —
  `temperature.land_albedo` (0.32 → 0.37), `climate.budyko_omega` (2.6 → 1.6),
  `erosion.erodibility` (×2), `erosion.sediment_diffusivity` (→ 10⁴) — plus
  `tectonics.initial_sediment.biogenic_pelagic`. `Epoch` sets solar constant and a prescribed
  greenhouse forcing (`temperature.greenhouse_forcing`, uniform W/m²). No carbon cycle.
- `src/temperature.rs`: EBM on a coarse mesh (subdivision 4, 2562 cells), 24 seasons, land
  albedo per coarse cell = `land_albedo` blended with ice by the ice-sheet cover
  (`ice_sheet_cover(t, p)`). A per-cell land albedo is a local change (the `land_albedo` vector is
  already per coarse cell). Outputs: `annual_mean`, `monthly`, `ice`, transports.
- `src/climate.rs`: moisture transport with Budyko/Fu runoff (`budyko_omega` global scalar, used
  in two places), per cell and month `precipitation`, `evaporation`, `potential_evaporation`,
  `runoff`, `monthly_precipitation`. Per-cell ω is a local change.
- `src/erosion.rs`: `ErosionParams::erodibility` global scalar used in the stream-power solver;
  per-cell K is possible (the solver already handles sediment vs bedrock erodibility per cell).
- `src/ocean.rs`: wind-driven gyre streamfunction and fluxes; `climate::surface_wind_stress`
  gives the zonal stress by latitude → Ekman upwelling (wind-stress curl, eastern boundaries,
  equatorial divergence) can be derived.
- `src/planet.rs`: `Planet::step(dt)` = tectonics → temperature (with last step's
  precipitation) → water cycle → erosion. `state_hash()` hashes per-cell elevation, crust,
  owner, precipitation, discharge, temperature (FNV-1a; #79/#85).
- Viewer: one surface colour scheme (elevation/ice) in `render::surface_color`, no layer
  switcher; `viewer/cell_info.rs` sends a 56-byte `CellInfo` per cell per update (2.3 MB at
  41k cells), tap/hover panel (#72, touch-friendly). Web: simulation in a Web Worker (#60),
  parameters as URL query, ~2× slower than native. PNG snapshots: elevation, plates,
  precipitation, temperature.
- Determinism: transcendental math via `src/math.rs`, hash maps via `crate::hash`, ordered
  iteration (#79, #87); RNG is seeded (`rand`-based in tectonics).
- Related open issue: #13 asteroid impacts (a natural source of mass extinctions later).

Code facts confirmed by the review: `temperature.land_albedo` is a *planetary* (top-of-atmosphere,
cloud-including) albedo per coarse cell (~440 km), so a surface contrast maps to about half of it
in the model (the presets' 0.32 → 0.37 for a surface change of ~0.1); the water cycle re-solves
only some months per step (4 solved months, one refreshed per step) and the temperature model runs
2 years per step, so the climate responds to a land-surface change over ~1–4 steps;
`erosion.runoff_discharge` is on by default, so Budyko ω also changes river discharge and erosion
(the remark in `presets.rs` that discharge follows precipitation is stale); with the default
(compensating) greenhouse forcing, the epoch hardly changes the absorbed sunlight.
