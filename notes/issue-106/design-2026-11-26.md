# Volcanism: design notes (issue #106, 2026-11-26)

Answer to Sebastian's questions on #106
(https://github.com/ISibboI/world-simulator/issues/106#issuecomment-7203050456). No code changes.
The planet is barren: no biosphere. It is Earth-like, not Earth. Errors that come from the
~110 km grid are accepted (#64), and this note says where they apply.

**Sources.** The literature values below are quoted **from memory**. Papers were not fetched for
this note. An independent reviewer agent checked the numbers, and its corrections are
included. Treat them as order-of-magnitude values, typically good to within a factor of ~2.
The references are listed at the end. The numbers that describe the model come from the code on
`main` at 1418167.

## 0. What the model already does (main, 1418167)

| Process | Where | Volcanism in it |

With an average of ~9 plumes alive (140 Myr mean lifetime / 15 Myr between births), the model is
close to the 7–10 "primary", deep-rooted hotspots of Courtillot et al. (2003). Earth's LIP rate is
~1 per 20–30 Myr for the large ones, more often if smaller ones count. The plume model is
therefore already a good clock for large igneous events (section 3).

## 1. Which volcanic features matter for relief at ~110 km cells?

Grid: 6 subdivisions = 40,962 cells of **12,450 km²** (spacing ~112 km). 5 subdivisions (web) =
10,242 cells of 49,800 km² (~223 km). One cell holds ~**4.4 × 10⁵ km³** of 35 km continental
crust, or ~**8.7 × 10⁴ km³** of 7 km oceanic crust.

**How added crust becomes relief.** At the cell scale, compensation is Airy-like, which is how
the model treats it. Adding basaltic crust of thickness h (ρ ≈ 2900 kg/m³, mantle 3300) raises
the surface by about:
- **0.12 h** on land (h (1 − ρc/ρm));
- **0.18 h** under water (h (ρm − ρc)/(ρm − ρw)).

Real volcanic edifices are partly held up by flexure, so their peaks stand much higher. Flexure
acts over ~150–300 km, so it is mostly sub-grid. The cell mean is what the model can show (#64).

**Answer to Q1:** yes. **Oceanic plateaus** and **hotspot chains** (and volcanic rifted margins)
are large enough to change relief at 6 (and 5) subdivisions. Continental flood basalts cover many
cells but change elevation by only ~100–200 m; their importance lies in lithology and climate.
Individual volcanoes are sub-grid, including Hawaii-size shields (one shield is about half a
cell). An Olympus Mons is not expected on a plate-tectonic Earth-like planet.

## 2. Small volcanoes: is tracking their crust worthwhile?

Per cell and per Myr step, against 35 km (continental) or 7 km (oceanic) of crust:

- **Intraplate fields, scattered stratovolcanoes, seamounts:** ~1–100 km³ per cell per Myr while
  active, which is ~0.1–8 m of crust per Myr (10⁻⁵–2 × 10⁻⁴ of the column). Such fields last a few
  Myr. Even a field active for 300 Myr would add < 1–2 km, and real ones add ≪ 100 m. That is far
  below the erosion and sedimentation the model already moves (tens of m/Myr on land). **Not
  worth tracking.**
- **Arc volcanism, in aggregate:** this one is large. Arcs grow by ~30–95 km³ per km of arc per
  Myr for intra-oceanic arcs (Dimalanta et al. 2002), and by ~20–40 km³ per km per Myr averaged
  over all arcs (Reymer & Schubert 1984). Per 112 km of arc that is 3,400–10,600 km³/Myr. Spread
  over a ~2-cell-wide belt, it is **130–420 m per Myr**, or **13–40 km per 100 Myr**: it builds
  continents. Only ~10–20% of it erupts at volcanoes. The rest is plutons and underplating
  (intrusive : extrusive ≈ 5–10 : 1; White et al. 2006).
  - **The model already adds this.** At default settings, one subducted sample adds 500 m over
    one cell, which is 6,200 km³. At 5 cm/yr of convergence, 0.45 samples per trench cell are
    subducted per Myr, so the model adds **~25 km³/km/Myr gross** (~50 at 10 cm/yr). Subduction
    erosion removes 300 m per sample at erosive margins.
  - That is at the low end of Earth's range, and plausible. The doc comment on
    `arc_crust_per_subduction` still mentions "≈ 800 m" from an older calibration. That
    discrepancy only touches the comment, not the physics, and is worth tidying.
  - One caveat: the model's arc addition scales linearly with convergence rate. Earth's
    per-km arc growth rates depend on it only weakly (summary item 5 in the archive already
    notes this as an open point).
  - Arc volcanoes therefore do not need separate tracking. They are the visible 10–20% of the
    arc source.
- **Mid-ocean ridges** (~21 km³/yr, 3 km²/yr × 7 km) are the largest source and are already
  modelled as new sea floor.

**Answer to Q2:** no. Small volcanoes should not be discrete objects, and their crust is not
worth booking. Keep three continuous, statistical sources: ridges (sea-floor production), arcs
(per subducted sample, as now) and plumes (new, section 5). What matters for climate is the
**magmatic flux by source**, which #154 needs for degassing anyway, and the **surface lithology**
the volcanism leaves behind (fresh mafic rock), not the individual volcanoes.

## 3. Frequency and size of large events (LIPs)

**What Earth shows.** Earth's record has about one LIP every 20–30 Myr if only the large ones
(≳ 10⁶ km³) are counted, and one every ~10 Myr if events down to ~10⁵ km³ are included (Ernst &
Buchan 2001; Bryan & Ernst 2008; Ernst 2014; Kasbohm et al. 2021).
- **Volumes** span ~10⁵–10⁸ km³. They are roughly log-normally distributed with a median near
  10⁶ km³, and their upper tail is the oceanic plateaus.
- **Eruption** of most of the volume takes ≲ 1–3 Myr, which is one to three model steps.
- **Source.** Most LIPs are attributed to plume heads (Richards et al. 1989; Campbell & Griffiths
  1990). Many precede continental breakup by a few Myr (Courtillot et al. 1999): Deccan–Seychelles,
  CAMP–Central Atlantic, Paraná–South Atlantic, NAIP–North Atlantic, Karoo–Ferrar.
- **Plume heads.** A starting plume head flattens to ~2000 km across below the lithosphere. It
  melts by decompression, and the melt volume depends strongly on how thin the lid is (the "lid
  effect"; White & McKenzie 1989; Ellam 1992):
  - under young oceanic or rifting lithosphere it gives 10⁷–10⁸ km³ (oceanic plateaus);
  - under thick cratonic lithosphere it gives 10⁶ km³ at the surface, plus a comparable or larger
    volume of sills and underplate.

**What is plausible for an Earth-like planet:** about the same rate as on Earth, one large event
per ~10–30 Myr. A hotter young mantle would mean more and bigger events (Archean komatiites, big
Proterozoic LIPs). The model has no mantle temperature history, so that effect would be a later
refinement.

**Approximation using the existing plume model (proposal):**
1. **Trigger.** Each plume's head emplaces one LIP when it arrives: when `head_growth()` reaches 1
   at age 10 Myr, or spread over ages ~5–10 Myr. With the default `plume_interval` of 15 Myr, this
   gives one LIP per ~15 Myr, which is Earth-like. Plumes inserted at the start of a run should
   either have their LIP or be treated as old, which is a choice to make. Because the trigger is
   tied to plume births, LIP timing, location (far from slab graveyards, by the existing
   candidate rule) and later rifting stay consistent: the head heats the lithosphere, the LIP
   erupts, and the plate rifts there ~10–20 Myr later (`rift_delay`). That sequence is the Earth
   sequence.
2. **Magnitude.** V = V₀ · (peak_flux / mean flux) · L(lid) · exp(σ ε), with ε ~ N(0, 1), σ ≈
   ln(10)/2, truncated to 10⁵–10⁸ km³.
   - V₀ ≈ 10⁶ km³ for continental and 10⁷ km³ for oceanic lithosphere.
   - L(lid) is a lid factor taken from the lithosphere's thermal or strength age, which the model
     already has (`Crust::strength_age`, the plume reheating). It runs from ~1 under thin, young
     or rifted lithosphere to ~0.1 under old cratons. On continents, part of the remainder goes
     to underplating, which thickens the crust below the surface.
3. **Footprint.** A radial Gaussian with a sigma of ~400–700 km around the plume, cut off at the
   head radius (`PLUME_HEAD_RADIUS` ≈ 1000 km), applied over one to three steps.
   - In the ocean it thickens the crust: +10–25 km at the centre gives a plateau (with a mafic
     density of ~3000 kg/m³), isostatically 2–4 km shallower, which then resists subduction through the existing `resists_subduction`
     logic.
   - On land it adds a mafic layer (`felsic` = 0) of ~0.5–2 km, plus an underplate of similar
     size.
   - The mass goes into a new ledger term (`Term::Plumes`).
4. **Tail (hotspot track).** After the head stage, the plume adds a continuous volume flux
   Q_tail ≈ 10⁴–2 × 10⁵ km³/Myr, scaled with `flux()`, at the cell above it each step. Plates
   move over the fixed plume, so age-progressive chains form by themselves, and through
   `has_rifted` a plume under a ridge also thickens the new crust (Iceland, ~25–40 km of crust).
   On continents, much of the tail flux stays at depth because the lid is thick (Yellowstone,
   Hoggar).

**Answer to Q3:** a stochastic process is already there. LIPs should come from plume-head arrival
in the existing mantle model rather than from a separate random clock. The model then gets the
Earth-like rate (~1 per 15 Myr by default), the right spatial pattern (far from subduction), the
link to rifting, and a size that depends on the lid (oceanic plateaus vs. traps). A log-normal
magnitude distribution with a median of ~10⁶ km³ continental and 10⁷ km³ oceanic covers the
observed 10⁵–10⁸ km³.

## 4. Climate ties (barren planet), at 1 Myr steps and ~110 km cells

| Tie | Time scale | Size | At Myr steps and 110 km? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **CO₂ from steady degassing** (ridges, arcs, plumes, metamorphic decarbonation) | continuous | sets the long-term greenhouse level together with weathering | **Yes: this is #154.** Sebastian decided that all mantle degassing will be approximated from the tectonics as global fluxes. Volcanism must feed #154 the per-source magmatic rates (ridge production, arc addition, plume/LIP volume), so the two must be designed together. |
| **CO₂ pulses from LIPs** | eruption over ≲ 1 Myr, in pulses of ≲ 100 kyr; the ocean takes up most of the CO₂ within ~10⁴ yr; drawdown by weathering over ~0.1–0.5 Myr (Colbourn et al. 2015) | Basalt carries ~0.1–0.5 wt% CO₂, i.e. ~3–15 Mt CO₂ per km³. A Deccan-size LIP (10⁶ km³) releases ~3,000–15,000 Gt CO₂. Averaged over a 1 Myr step, that is only **0.003–0.015 Gt CO₂/yr, ~1–5% of background volcanic degassing** (~0.3 Gt/yr; Gerlach 2011). Earth's LIP warming spikes of a few °C (end-Permian, Deccan) come from sub-Myr pulses, and from **thermogenic** CO₂/CH₄ where sills bake sedimentary carbon (Siberia; Svensen et al. 2009). **Oceanic plateaus** are the exception: 5 × 10⁷ km³ in ~3 Myr is ~0.05–0.25 Gt/yr, comparable to background. | **Mostly no.** At Myr steps a continental LIP's own magmatic CO₂ warms the step mean by well under 1 °C. Oceanic plateaus and an optional thermogenic factor (LIP over thick sediment) can give a noticeable multi-step warming. This is a **later #154 extension**: add the step's LIP volume (× a thermogenic factor) as an extra degassing source. |
| **SO₂ and sulfate aerosol cooling** | 1–3 years per eruption (Pinatubo: ~20 Mt SO₂, −0.5 °C for ~2 yr). LIP pulses: decades to centuries of repeated eruptions, possibly several °C of cooling during a pulse, recovering within decades of its end (Schmidt et al. 2016). | large but intermittent | **No.** It averages out over a 1 Myr step. The only defensible Myr-mean effect is a small cooling (≲ 1 °C) during LIP steps, and Earth data do not constrain it well. Leave it out, or add an optional forcing term later. |
| **Albedo of fresh basalt** | fresh lava: surface albedo ~0.05–0.1 against ~0.2–0.35 for felsic/desert surfaces; it reddens and lightens with weathering over 10³–10⁵ yr | The model's `land_albedo` of 0.32 is planetary, with clouds and atmosphere, so a dark surface changes it by perhaps 0.03–0.08 locally. Even 10⁶ km² of LIP is 0.2% of the planet. | **Negligible globally.** A barren planet has no vegetation term, though, so a lithology-dependent land albedo (from `felsic` and sediment cover) would be a cheap general refinement. It is not specific to volcanism. |
| **Weathering of fresh basalt** | 10⁴–10⁶ yr | Basalt weathers ~5–10× faster than granite per unit area. Basalts cover ~5% of today's continental area yet account for ~30% of CO₂ consumption by silicate weathering (Dessert et al. 2003; Gaillardet et al. 1999). LIPs and arcs in the tropics are proposed drivers of long-term cooling (Deccan, CAMP; Franklin LIP before Snowball Earth, Cox et al. 2016; Macdonald et al. 2019). | **Yes, and probably the largest lasting climate effect of volcanism.** #154's silicate weathering should scale with **surface lithology**, the mafic fraction (1 − `felsic`) of exposed crust, as well as with temperature, runoff and erosion. LIP warming then gives way to cooling over ~1–10 Myr as the fresh basalt weathers. |
| **Orographic effects of volcanic relief** | as long as the relief lasts | Plateaus, hotspot islands and volcanic margins are cell-scale relief. The model's orographic precipitation sees them automatically. | **Yes, automatically**, once phases 1 and 2 add the crust. Individual volcano peaks are sub-grid (#64). |
| Volcanic heat, water, ash | — | Geothermal heat ~0.09 W/m², against ~240 W/m² absorbed sunlight. Magmatic water degassing is small against the ocean on 100 Myr scales and is balanced by regassing at subduction zones. Ash is fertiliser (relevant for the biosphere, #90). | No. |

**Answer to Q4.** On Myr steps the climate ties are, in order:
1. CO₂ degassing from tectonic magmatic fluxes, which is #154.
2. Faster weathering of fresh mafic rock, which needs lithology in #154's weathering.
3. CO₂ from oceanic plateaus and thermogenic LIP carbon, as a #154 extension. A continental LIP's own magmatic CO₂ is only ~1–5% of background degassing when averaged over a Myr.
4. Relief from plateaus and chains, through orographic precipitation, which follows automatically.

SO₂ aerosols and basalt albedo are below the resolution of the model or too small to matter.

**#154 (carbon cycle) must be kept in mind when volcanism is added** (Sebastian's request on
this issue). Degassing in #154 is approximated from tectonic fluxes: ridge production,
subduction return at arcs, plumes, and metamorphic/collision decarbonation.
- Any new magmatic source added here (plume heads and LIPs, hotspot tails, thicker crust at
  plume-fed ridges) must be booked so that #154 can read it as a flux, with no double counting.
- LIP degassing and mafic-lithology weathering are the two extensions of #154 that depend on this
  issue.
- If volcanism lands first, it should expose per-step magmatic volumes by source. If #154 lands
  first, its plume-degassing term should take the plume flux in a form that LIP volumes can
  replace later.

## 5. Integration proposal (phases)

The efforts below are rough agent-days, including tests, CI and before/after seed runs.

| Phase | What | Changes | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Magmatic flux diagnostics** | Report per-step volumes for ridge production, arc addition and (later) plume additions in the ledger/statistics output. | No change to physics. Gives #154 its degassing inputs and #110 its activity map. | ~0.5 day. Can be part of #154. |
| **1. Plume-head LIPs** | At head arrival, emplace one LIP with a log-normal volume and a lid factor (section 3). Ocean: crustal thickening becomes a plateau. Land: a mafic layer plus underplate. New ledger term, plus tests for mass conservation and for the plateau resisting subduction. | ~1 LIP per 15 Myr. Oceanic plateaus over tens to ~150 cells, 2–4 km shallower and buoyant: they jam trenches, accrete as terranes and add continental growth. Flood-basalt provinces: little relief, but mafic lithology for #154. Changes continental-crust statistics, so it needs before/after runs. | ~2–4 days |
| **2. Hotspot tracks and plume–ridge crust** | Tail flux added each step at the cell above each plume (scaled by `flux()`, Q ≈ 10⁴–2 × 10⁵ km³/Myr). A plume near a ridge thickens the new sea floor. | **4. Activity view, with #110** | Map of recent magmatic additions (ridges, arcs, active plumes, LIPs of the last few Myr), plus seismic proxies from boundary rates. | Display only. | Part of #110 (~1–2 days) |

**Not worth modelling** (sub-grid per #64, or averaged out at Myr steps):
- individual stratovolcanoes, cinder cones, monogenetic fields, calderas;
- individual seamounts (except plume-fed chains);
- eruption frequency and VEI statistics;
- SO₂/sulfate aerosols (at most an optional mean forcing during LIP steps);
- ash fall;
- volcanic heat and water;
- flexural moats and arches around edifices (~150–300 km wavelength, at the grid scale; Airy at
  the cell scale is the accepted simplification).

**Grid caveat to explain (#64).** A shield like Mauna Loa or Kilimanjaro stands 4–6 km above its
surroundings, but at 110 km it shows up only as a cell-mean rise of ≲ 1–4 km over the hotspot
track. At 5 subdivisions the rise is about half of that. Peaks are not shown, and neither are
the moats around them.

**Order:** Phase 0 with #154. Phase 1 and then 2 before or after #154, booked as flux sources
per section 4. Phase 3 after both. #51/#54 (plume rifting, aulacogens) are independent of this,
but phase 1's footprint should use the same plume-head geometry (`PLUME_HEAD_RADIUS`).
